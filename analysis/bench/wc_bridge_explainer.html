A bridge instead of the weights

The prover shows that it ran the permitted model and nothing else. Until now every proof reprocessed all 400.7 billion weights from scratch, which took 42 minutes, even though the weights are the same from one run to the next. Now the weights are fixed once by a separate checkable structure, called the model registration. Each proof checks against it at 40 random points, and that check takes 10 minutes. Forging the weights and passing the check has a probability of 2 to the power minus 40, about one chance in a trillion.

The weights do not change from proof to proof, yet each proof paid for them again, 42 minutes for 400 billion numbers. That price is now paid once, when the model is registered, and each proof gets away with a 40 point check that takes 10 minutes.

How it works

The check ties the numbers the proof actually uses to what was registered, so one cannot be swapped while the other stays.

A worked example

Take a model of three weights, 3, 1 and 4. Real sizes are not needed, the whole mechanism is visible on three numbers.

Registration

The weights become coefficients of a polynomial, and its fingerprint is published. The fingerprint does not reveal the weights, and it does not let them be swapped quietly.

F(x) = 3 + 1·x + 4·x²

An honest proof

The verifier names a random point, say x = 2, after the prover has committed to everything. Both sides evaluate, and the values agree.

F(2) = 3 + 2 + 16 = 21 ✓

An attempted forgery

The prover quietly changes the last weight from 4 to 5, to pass a different model off as the registered one. At the point 2 the values disagree and the check fails.

3 + 2 + 20 = 25 ≠ 21 ✗

Why it cannot be guessed

The difference between the real and the forged model is itself a polynomial, and it is zero at only a handful of points. A forgery passes only by landing exactly on one.

difference = 1·x², zero only at x = 0

At real sizes

The polynomial has degree up to 16 384, there are 32 768 points to choose from, and 40 are drawn after everything is committed. A forgery has to match at all 40 at once.

about 1 in 1 128 000 000 000

Why the weights stay secret

1024 random numbers are added on top of the real coefficients. Each checked point reveals one equation about them, and while there are fewer than 1024 equations the weights cannot be recovered.

40 points per proof, enough for 25

The order of events in one proof

1 · Register the model, once

All the weights are cut into blocks and turned into polynomials, the weights themselves plus 1024 random masks on top, and the masks are what stop the checks from revealing the weights. All of it is expanded to 32768 points and reduced to one fingerprint, which becomes the public name of the model.

400.7 billion weights → one fingerprint

2 · A random number

The prover first publishes all the results of the run. Only then does a random number appear, which could not have been known in advance, so the results cannot be fitted to the future check. One such number serves all 72 matrix multiplications at once.

random number = hash of everything published

3 · Squeeze the weights into one column

The prover publishes the weights folded by that random number, one value where a whole row used to be. This is exactly the quantity the rest of the proof uses, not a separate copy made for the check, and a substitution is caught at build time. It costs nothing extra, because it rides along with a pass that already reads every weight.

folded weights and folded masks

4 · Draw the 40 check points

More random numbers appear now, one per block of weights so that all blocks mix into one, and 40 distinct points where the check will happen. The prover hands over the values of its mixture at those points. There are 40 of them because each point buys about one bit of assurance and 40 give one chance in a trillion. More is possible, but every point spends part of the registration, and at 64 points one registration lasts 16 proofs instead of 25.

40 points out of 32768

5 · The check

The verifier takes the same 40 points from the registration and sees whether the values agree. Lying about the weights works only if the lie happens to match at all 40 points at once, and the points were drawn after the prover had committed to everything.

about 1 in 1 128 000 000 000

What one registration is worth

Each proof spends 40 points out of 1024, and that is true whatever the request length, a thousand tokens and a million cost the same. While fewer than 1024 are spent the weights cannot be recovered. So one registration lasts 25 proofs, after which the model is registered again with fresh masks. The prover tracks the remainder itself and refuses to work when it runs out.

1024 ÷ 40 = 25 proofs of any length

Where the time goes, measured

Time per proof = 5 model runs: 2 145 s by the model, 3 612 s observed ← the protocol needs five passes, the largest single cost + check against the registration: 580 s ← reading 40 points, the folding rides along and costs nothing + correctness checks: 677 + 93 + 23 + 18 s ← unchanged + packing, loading the model, writing the file: 602 + 59 + 207 s ← plus 700 s of fixed protocol allowances + weight reprocessing 2 423 + weight opening 74 s ← gone (registering the model 633 s once, the next 25 proofs pay nothing for it)
85 minutes against 117 before the change. In the real run the model runs took longer than the model predicts, 60 minutes instead of 36, and they now set the pace rather than the weights. The structural change is that only the check scales with the size of the model, everything else scales with the length of the request.

What a model run costs here

A proving run is the same forward pass the model would do anyway, with two differences. Every number is an exact 64-bit integer instead of a low precision float, because a proof cannot round. And every intermediate value is written down, since that is what gets proved, which is about 7 recorded numbers per operation output at these shapes.

The protocol needs five such runs, one per stage of the exchange, and the run cannot be stored between stages because at this size it is around 760 GB. On our card one run took 960 seconds cold and 564 seconds afterwards, so five runs are 60 minutes. Plain inference on the same card was not measured, so the honest split is this. The 60 minutes are the proving runs, not the model doing ordinary work, and the part the protocol adds is the exact arithmetic, the recording, and the repetition.
